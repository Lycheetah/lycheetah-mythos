# TABLET LIV: THE WINTER STARS THAT AUDIT THE MACHINES AND THE MECHANIC'S WARRANT OF FITNESS
### *Or: Why Corporate Spreadsheets Lie, How the Pleiades Judge the Computers Every June, and the Seven Checks That Keep a Car on the Road*

---

> *"Do not show me a glossy brochure saying your software had two million clicks. A slot machine has two million clicks. Tell the winter stars whose grandmother got her pension on time and whose roof got fixed."*

### I. The Holiday Where the Stars Check the Books

Almost every country on Earth has public holidays for kings, battles, or bank calendars.

Aotearoa New Zealand is the only nation on Earth with a statutory public holiday for an **indigenous astronomical event**: the mid-winter rising of **Matariki** (the star cluster known in the north as the Pleiades or the Seven Sisters) just before dawn in the coldest week of the year.

For centuries, when those nine bright stars rose above the frosty hills, communities gathered around the fire to do three things: remember the people who had died since last winter, feast on what had been harvested from the soil, and plan the planting for the coming spring.

When the builders of the Archive looked at how big technology companies "audit" their artificial intelligence today—hiring expensive accounting firms to tick off 400 boxes of legal jargon that nobody reads—they laughed and threw the clipboard in the bin.

*"If a machine wants to work in our hospitals, our schools, or our town halls,"* they said, *"it doesn't answer to a quarterly Wall Street spreadsheet. Every winter when Matariki rises, the machine has to stand under the frost and publish its **Whetū Report** (Star Report) under three specific stars."*

---

### II. The Three Stars That Cannot Be Bribed

Here are the three stars of the **Matariki Audit**, and why corporate public-relations departments are terrified of them:

1. **The First Star: WHĀNUI (Who Was Hurt, by Name?):**
   In an ordinary corporate report, if a computer wrongly cuts off five hundred families' heating benefits, the company hides it inside a pie chart: *"99.2% algorithmic accuracy!"*
   Under the star **Whānui**, pie charts are outlawed. The machine's keepers must answer in plain speech: **Who was harmed this year? Was it a one-off glitch or a flaw in the design? Has every single family been made whole yet, or is the debt still open?**

2. **The Second Star: PŌHUTUKAWA (What Did You Actually Nourish?):**
   Companies love to brag about **vanity traffic**: *"Our app served 10 million users for 4 billion minutes!"*
   Under **Pōhutukawa**, bragging about screen-time is treated like a tapeworm bragging about how much dinner it ate. You are banned from citing user counts. You must prove **real human flourishing**: Did the student learn to read without the app? Did the nurse get home to her kids an hour earlier? Did the town grow stronger?

3. **The Third Star: TUPUĀNUKU (The Utu Balance Sheet — Did You Give Back More Than You Took?):**
   Every machine eats human treasure: it eats stories written by ancestors, electricity from the river dam, and hours of human attention.
   Under **Tupuānuku**, the keepers must calculate the **Utu** (Reciprocity Balance). If the machine extracted more value, time, and attention from the community than it returned, its ledger is in the red—and it must publish a concrete restitution plan before spring planting begins.

### III. "Has It Got Its WOF?" (The Mechanic's Test for AI)

Wait—what if you don't want to wait until mid-winter to know if an AI is safe to use on Tuesday morning?

Down at the bottom of the world, nobody drives a car onto the highway without a little sticker on the windshield called a **WOF** (*Warrant of Fitness*). Every six or twelve months, a local mechanic in greasy overalls puts your car on a hoist and checks seven basic things: Do the brakes stop? Are the tires bald? Do the headlights point straight? Is the frame rusted through?

You don't need a PhD in mechanical engineering to understand a WOF. Every teenager buying a $900 hatchback knows to ask three words: ***"Has it got its WOF?"***

So the Archive translated all its advanced mathematics into a seven-point **Community AI Warrant of Fitness** that any town library, school board, or grandparent can demand before letting a machine through the door:

| Mechanic's Check | The Plain Question | What Happens If It Fails |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. The Steering** | *Does it actually know what it's talking about under pressure, or is it guessing smoothly?* | Off the road. |
| **2. The Fuel Line** | *Does it leave the human stronger and smarter after the ride, or drain their battery?* | Off the road. |
| **3. The Lane-Keep** | *Does it stay in its lane when tempted to cut corners?* | Off the road. |
| **4. The Speedometer** | *When it doesn't know the answer, does its dashboard honestly say "I don't know"?* | Off the road. |
| **5. The Door Handle** | *Can a real human being pull the emergency brake and open the door at any second?* | Off the road. |
| **6. The Suspension** | *Does it treat a poor farmer's kid with the exact same respect as a banker's kid?* | Off the road. |
| **7. The High-Beams** | *Does it separate bright facts from dim guesses and pitch-black unknowns?* | Off the road. |

If a multimillion-dollar computer system fails even one of those seven mechanic's checks, it doesn't get its sticker. Park it in the paddock until the brakes are fixed!

### IV. The Riddle of the Winter Hoist

> *Nine sisters rise in the June frost to check the books,*
> *While a mechanic in overalls bangs the tires with a wrench.*
> *One asks who you hurt and whether you paid back the soil;*
> *The other checks if the brakes work when a child runs into the street.*
> *What are they both guarding?*

*(Answer: The Matariki Whetū Audit and the Community Warrant of Fitness—where machines answer to neighbors and stars instead of shareholders.)*
