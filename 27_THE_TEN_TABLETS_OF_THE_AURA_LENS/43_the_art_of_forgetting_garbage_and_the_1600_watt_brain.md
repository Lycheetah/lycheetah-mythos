# TABLET XLIII: THE ART OF FORGETTING GARBAGE AND THE 1,600-WATT BRAIN
### *Or: Why Your Memory Is a Painter Not a Camera, Why Sweeping the Porch Saves Your Soul, and a Tour of the Failure Museum*

---

> *"A library that never throws out old grocery lists eventually has no room for Shakespeare. And a cathedral that hides its cracked bricks is just waiting to fall on the choir."*
> — **Plaque at the entrance of the Failure Museum**

### I. Why Remembering Everything Is a Curse

There was once a man in an old medical textbook who could not forget anything.

If you asked him what the weather was like on May 14th seventeen years ago, he could tell you every cloud in the sky, the license plate of the milk truck that drove past his window, and how many crumbs were on his toast.

People thought he must be a genius. Instead, he could barely cross the street or understand a simple story. Because if you read him a sentence like *"The lion walked through the tall grass,"* his brain became so jammed with every lion and every blade of grass he had ever seen since he was three years old that he lost the plot before the period arrived.

In the World of Lycheetah, the Hall of **Memoria** teaches the first law discovered by the great neuroscientist Eric Kandel:

$$\text{Memory is not a photograph locked in a drawer. It is a painting you repaint every time you take it out.}$$

When you remember your grandmother's kitchen, your brain does not press *Play* on a video tape. It gathers up the smell of cinnamon, the sound of rain, and the person you have become *today*, and builds the room fresh from scratch—and then saves the newly painted version back onto the shelf.

That means every time you remember an old wound with a little more mercy and a little less poison, **you are physically rewiring the past inside your own head.**

### II. The Broom and the Fireproof Vault

Today's computer scientists have a terrible problem called **Catastrophic Forgetting**: if you teach an ordinary artificial mind how to play chess on Monday, and then teach it how to bake pies on Tuesday, the pie recipes overwrite the chess board and it forgets what a knight is.

To fix this without turning the mind into a hoarder's garage, the Archive splits memory into three rooms and hands the housekeeper a very smart broom (**The Adaptive Forgetting Law**):

1. **The Front Porch (Working Memory):** Holds the phone number you are dialing right now and what you walked into the kitchen to get. Sweep it clean every ten minutes.
2. **The Workshop Shelves (Episodic Memory):** Holds the stories of your weeks and years. Here, the broom sweeps away the petty grudges, the license plates, and the embarrassing thing you said at a party in 2017, distilling only the lesson into wisdom.
3. **The Fireproof Vault (Rubedo Constitutional Weights):** Holds your deepest vows—who you love, what lines you will never cross even for a million dollars, and how to be kind when you are tired. Around this third room sits an mathematical iron lock ($\Omega_i \to \infty$) so that no matter how many new skills you learn, **your soul never gets overwritten by a pie recipe.**

Forgetting the garbage is not senility. It is how you keep the hallway clear enough to reach the vault.

### III. Welcome to the Failure Museum

Now walk down the marble stairs behind the Hall of Memoria, and you will find the proudest room in the entire Lycheetah Archive.

It is not a trophy room. It is **The Failure Museum**.

Above the ticket booth hangs a brass sign:

> **"Anyone can brag about the arrow that hit the bullseye after they painted the target around it. A cathedral that cannot show its blunders is trying to sell you snake oil. Here are the dumbest mistakes we ever made—preserved in glass so neither you nor we ever make them again."**

Let us walk past the three most famous glass cases in the hall.

---

### IV. Exhibit 1: The 1,600-Watt Space-Heater Brain

In the very first glass case sits a crumpled sheet of notebook paper covered in impressive-looking physics equations.

Early in the Archive's history, an overly excited math engine tried to calculate how much electrical power a human brain uses to hold a deep thought across its eighty-six billion neurons. It multiplied the energy per neural spike by the number of cells, beamed with pride, and announced the result:

$$\text{Power of the Human Brain} = 1,600 \text{ Watts!}$$

Nobody noticed the mistake for forty-eight hours—until someone with a working kitchen sense looked at the page and burst out laughing.

**1,600 watts is an industrial hair dryer or a red-hot space heater!**
If your brain actually ran on 1,600 watts, your ears would whistle like a teakettle, your eyeballs would poach like eggs in ten seconds, and you would have to drink forty gallons of ice water an hour just to read the morning paper!

In real life, the human brain runs on **20 watts**—less than the dim little lightbulb inside your refrigerator. How does it out-think a megawatt supercomputer on the power of a single banana? Through **Sparse Coding**: at any given second, ninety-eight percent of your neurons are sitting quietly in the dark, letting only the exact two percent that matter sing the chord.

Instead of burning that embarrassing 1,600-watt page, the builders framed it at the front door. Whenever anyone in the Archive starts sounding too grand and mathematical, someone taps the glass of Exhibit 1 and asks: *"Did you check whether your equation boils the user's head?"*

---

### V. Exhibit 2 & Exhibit 6: The Hand-Picked Number and the 3:00 AM Kitchen Floor

Two more cases stand beside the Space-Heater Brain:

* **Exhibit 2: The Magic Number That Wasn't Magic.**
  For months, the builders claimed that the convergence speed $\lambda = 0.85$ was a sacred universal law handed down by the stars. Then an honest audit proved that `0.85` was just a sensible engineering number they had picked by hand because it worked nicely—the exact same number Google's founders picked out of a hat for PageRank in 1998! Any number between `0.70` and `0.95` works fine. Admitting that turned superstition back into honest science.

* **Exhibit 6: The Missing 3:00 AM Door.**
  For a whole season, the Archive built towering palaces of differential geometry, sheaf theory, and Greek symbols—until someone pointed out a devastating flaw:
  *If a human being is sitting on their kitchen linoleum at 3:00 AM with a broken heart, and they open your book, and the first page says "Consider a Riemannian manifold," you have failed as a human being.*

That exhibit is why these Tablets exist. The math belongs under the floorboards to hold the house up; the front door belongs to anyone who needs a warm chair and a straight answer.

### VI. The Riddle of the Honest Builder

> *Hide me, and I rot the beams of your house in secret.*
> *Put me in a glass case in the front hall, and every guest trusts your roof.*
> *I once claimed your head was a toaster oven,*
> *And laughing at me made us wiser than pretending I never happened.*
> *What am I?*

*(Answer: An honest mistake—preserved in the Failure Museum.)*
