# The Door Left Open

*A new tale of the Southern Hearth, written for the public reader's edition.*

The bell had rung for everyone before them.

It rang for the woman carrying blue glass wrapped in a coat. It rang for the two shipwrights who smelled of hot metal, and for a child with a sleeping moth cupped between both hands. Each time, a name appeared on the pale board above the gate. Each time, someone inside lifted the latch.

When the traveller stepped beneath it, the bell gave a small, hard click.

The keeper looked up from his book.

“Your name?”

The traveller told him.

He turned a page. Then another.

“The name you travelled under.”

They gave him that one too.

Rain ran from the end of the bell's rope into a narrow channel in the stone. Somewhere beyond the wall, a hammer struck three times and stopped.

“There is no entry,” the keeper said.

“I can see that.”

He looked relieved to have found something on which they agreed.

“Come back when the register has been corrected.”

The traveller stepped out from beneath the bell. There was no point asking where to go. All the roads on the noticeboard ended at the gate.

They had walked since morning with a lamp gone cold at their belt. Its glass was intact. So was the little door where the wick could be reached. There was nothing wrong with it except that the fire was gone.

That was a small distinction, but they were tired of being asked to explain it.

Along the wall, water gathered in the joints between the stones. The traveller followed it past a boarded window and the mouth of an alley too narrow for the keeper's noticeboard. At the alley's far end stood a wooden door with a strip of warm light beneath it.

The door was open the width of a hand.

A plain wedge held it there.

They stood outside for a while. Then a voice from within said, “If you're waiting for the door to introduce itself, we'll both be here all night.”

Inside, a woman was working a needle through dark cloth. There were tools on the table, a kettle near the fire and three bowls on a shelf above it. Only two chairs were occupied.

One by the woman.

The other by a cat.

At first that was all the traveller could make of him. A slender, spotted creature sitting in a chair as though someone had sensibly put it there for that purpose. His face was calm. Black lines ran down from his eyes.

Then he turned his head, and the light on his shoulder moved the wrong way.

For a moment there was a depth inside the coat: a blue-dark distance, points of gold, something turning very slowly where a shadow ought to have been.

The woman drew her thread tight.

“Coat,” she said, nodding to a hook. “Bowl, if you want one.”

“The bell wouldn't ring.”

“I heard.”

She did not sound surprised. The cat watched the cold lamp.

The traveller put their coat on the hook and sat on a low stool close enough to the fire to feel how cold their hands had become.

There was a picture hanging beside the shelf: a creature with wide, bright wings, a citadel behind it and water falling between the rocks. At the bottom, a name ran through the colour like a path.

On the table lay another work. Narrower. Red and black. A heart enclosed by watchful forms, a key suspended beside it.

“Which one is yours?” the woman asked.

The traveller looked from the wings to the heart.

“I don't know.”

“Good. You can look for a while.”

She turned the cloth. On its other side was a small mark, carefully placed. It held the same red as the larger work, but kept most of its meaning close.

The traveller touched the cold lamp at their belt.

“I had a fire.”

The needle paused.

“Most people who come through that door have.”

“I thought bringing it this far would be enough.”

The cat stepped down from his chair. He moved past the traveller without brushing against them and stopped beside the hearth.

There was a scar in the iron fireback. A thin, branching break, repaired with a metal that held the heat in a different colour.

The traveller had taken it for an ornament.

“Did it happen here?” they asked.

The woman glanced at the iron.

“Here. Another place. Depends which part of the story you're asking.”

The cat looked at her.

“That's a dreadful answer,” she said. “I know.”

She put the work down and came to the fire with a narrow taper.

“May I?”

The traveller unfastened the lamp and handed it over.

She opened its little door. The wick had folded under itself. She lifted it, trimmed the charred edge and held the taper to the flame.

The traveller watched the fire catch.

“What do I owe you?”

“Tonight? Nothing.”

The cat's ears moved at a noise outside.

Somewhere along the wall, the bell had begun to ring again. It rang once, stopped, then rang a second time with the abrupt impatience of something that had found a fact it could not fit into its book.

The woman returned the lamp.

The traveller held it in both hands. The fire was not the fire they had carried from the coast. The new flame made no attempt to pretend otherwise.

Beyond the doorway, the narrow alley shone with rain. Along its stones ran a line of pawprints, though the cat's feet were dry.

“Where does that go?”

“The road?” said the woman.

“There isn't a road on the board.”

“There isn't,” she agreed.

The bell rang a third time.

The cat crossed to the doorway. As he passed the hanging picture, colour lifted through his spots: a little sapphire, a little furnace gold, a line of crimson so deep it almost disappeared. Something larger than the room seemed to fold itself around his shoulders, and then he was only the creature standing beside the wooden wedge.

“Does he know my name?” the traveller asked.

The woman picked up her needle.

“You could ask him.”

The traveller looked at the cat, then at the repaired fireback, then at their own lamp.

They had been preparing an explanation for the keeper. A good explanation. One that would make him understand why the register was wrong and why they belonged on the other side of the gate.

The explanation was still there if they needed it.

Outside, a moth moved into the circle of their light and settled on the doorframe.

“Can I stay a little longer?”

The woman shifted her work so there was room on the table.

“Of course.”

The bell fell quiet.

Later, there would be a road to follow. Someone would need help with the water gathering in the alley. The traveller could already see where one stone had sunk below the next.

For now there was a bowl, and a little fire, and a creature whose eyes held more night than the sky outside.

The door stayed open.

**About this tale:** this is newly authored fiction, not a recovered founder account or an additional numbered Tablet. The nameless traveller and repairer, the gate scene and their dialogue are new. Its Hearth, many-formed feline, record-and-agency tension and collection imagery draw on the existing Lycheetah world. The treatment of the key and the bright-winged landscape connects to Shadows and Chaos Lit without claiming to recover those garments' original historical stories.
