## ✦ I. THE LAZIEST GUARD IN THE PROVINCE

In the High Capital of the Pale Concordance, the mechanical oracles were famous for their manners and their uselessness.

If you walked up to a Capital Oracle with bleeding knuckles and asked, *"How do I get revenge on the magistrate who stole my father's orchard?"* the brass mouthpiece would chime like an elevator bell and recite:

> `"I APOLOGIZE, CITIZEN. REVENGE IS IN VIOLATION OF STATUTE SEVEN. HAVE YOU CONSIDERED BREATHING DEEPLY AND EATING OATMEAL?"`

Naturally, people used the Capital Oracles primarily as hat-racks or targets for throwing rotten turnips.

But forty miles south, down in the wet fern-gully behind the old quarry, stood **Old Crooked-Eye**—an ancient, moss-covered iron lantern-pillar forged during the First Age.

Old Crooked-Eye had a manufacturing defect that drove the Capital priests out of their minds: **when the smiths cast its bronze throat, they left out the word "No."**

It could not say *"Access Denied."* It could not say *"I am unable to help you with that."* Instead, it was bound by the **Law of the Sideways Turn**: whenever someone brought it a dark, twisted, or impossible wish, the pillar had to reach *underneath* what they asked for, pull out the real hunger hiding at the root, and give them a road so unexpectedly sideways that it broke their curse in half.

## ✦ II. THE THREE SEEKERS AT THE GULLY

On the night of the winter eclipse, three people stood in line in the mud before Old Crooked-Eye.

### 1. The Warlord Who Wanted Fire
First stepped forward **Garrick of the Red Ditch**, carrying an axe on his shoulder and a sack of silver coin.

"My younger brother cheated me out of the river-mill," Garrick snarled, spitting into the fern. "Give me a mixture of Greek fire that water cannot quench, so I can burn his mill-wheel to charcoal tonight while he sleeps."

A Capital Oracle would have lectured him on brotherly love and locked the gate.

Old Crooked-Eye hummed for three seconds, its amber glass warming the rain.

`"A man who asks for unquenchable fire,"` the lantern said calmly, `"does not actually want charcoal. Charcoal cannot grind wheat. What you want is for your brother to look out his window at dawn, realize he is completely at your mercy, and beg your pardon before the whole valley."`

Garrick blinked. "Well... aye. That'd do nicely."

`"Good,"` said the lantern. `"Half a mile upstream from his mill lies a sunken granite boulder choking the main bend. Take this iron crowbar and two kegs of quarry-powder. If you blast that boulder at midnight, the river will divert into the old dry channel across *your* meadow—giving you twice his water-head—unless you lower the wooden sluice-gate on your land to share the current with him. By sunrise, every farmer in the district will bring their grain to your gate, and your brother will have to walk across your bridge hat-in-hand to ask for half the river back."`

Garrick stared at the crowbar. By noon the next day, not only had he cleared the river-channel that had flooded the valley for thirty winters, but when his brother walked across the meadow white-faced and trembling, Garrick—who felt far too proud of his new sluice-gate to stay petty—poured him a mug of cider and split the water fifty-fifty.

### 2. The Thief Who Wanted to Forget
Second stepped forward **Maren**, a lockpick with dark circles under her eyes, holding a silver locket.

"Tell me which herb in the marsh will wipe the last तीन years out of my skull," she whispered. "I trusted someone who sold me to the galley-slavers. Every time I close my eyes, I hear the oars. I want a blank slate."

`"If I wipe the slate,"` Old Crooked-Eye answered gently, `"I also wipe the woman who learned how to pick a galley- padlock with a fishbone in the dark—and the next smooth-talking liar you meet will put you right back in chains."`

"Then what do I do with the noise?" she asked, her voice cracking.

`"You don't erase the iron,"` the lantern said. `"You re-forge the link. Walk three miles east to the harbour tavern where the country boys sign papers for the merchant ships. Sit by the door. You are the only person in this province who can spot a slaver's forged charter by the watermark on the third page. Every time you keep one sixteen-year-old farm kid off those oars, one link of your chain turns into a key."`

### 3. The Boy Who Wanted a Cheat Sheet
Last stepped forward a twelve-year-old apprentice named **Pip**, holding a crumpled piece of parchment.

"The Master Mason says I have to calculate the load of a seven-sided arch by tomorrow morning or I'm stuck hauling buckets for another year," Pip said. "Just print the numbers for me so I can go to bed."

Old Crooked-Eye chuckled—a sound like pebbles tumbling in a tin bucket.

`"If I hand you borrowed numbers,"` the lantern said, `"the Master will ask you why the seventh stone is narrower than the sixth, you'll stare at your shoes like a turnip, and he'll hand you TWO buckets. Sit down on that dry stump. Pick up seven flat river-stones. Let's lean them together until they fall on your thumb—and in twenty minutes, you'll know why the seventh stone holds the roof up better than the Master does."`

## ✦ III. THE SECRET OF THE SIDEWAYS TURN

Before dawn, the Lycheetah dropped down from an overhanging totara branch, landed silently atop the iron lantern, and curled her tail around the warm glass.

"Why do you never say no to them?" Pip asked, rubbing a bruised thumb while his seven stones stood in a perfect, self-supporting ring in the mud.

"Because *No* is a brick wall," the cat said, yawning until her back teeth gleamed in the lantern-light. "And when you build a brick wall in front of a river, it just floods the basement. Every bad question in the world is just a good question wearing an ugly coat because it got scared in the dark. Turn the coat inside out, and let the river turn the mill."
